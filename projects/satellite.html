Methodology & Technical Approach

Identifying land cover types from satellite imagery is a fundamental challenge in environmental monitoring — with applications ranging from agricultural assessment to deforestation tracking. This project applied unsupervised machine learning to classify areas of forage availability directly from raw satellite images, without requiring labelled training data.

The core insight was that different land cover types — healthy vegetation, sparse biomass, and barren land — have distinct spectral signatures that manifest as visually separable colour clusters in satellite imagery. By using K-Means clustering to partition the colour space, these regions could be automatically identified and masked.

01

Image Acquisition & Preprocessing

Satellite images of the target geographic area were obtained and loaded into the processing pipeline using Python. Images were converted from their raw format into a suitable colour space (RGB / HSV) for clustering, and reshaped from 2D image arrays into flat pixel matrices to make them compatible with sklearn's clustering API.

02

K-Means Clustering

The K-Means algorithm was applied to the pixel matrix to partition pixels into k distinct colour clusters. The value of k was selected empirically by evaluating the separation between clusters using inertia and visual inspection of the segmented output. Each cluster effectively represented a distinct land cover class based on its dominant colour signature.

03

Mask Generation

Once clusters were assigned, masks were generated by selecting the clusters that corresponded to biomass-rich areas (green, dense vegetation) versus those corresponding to barren or forage-depleted land. Binary masks were applied back to the original image to produce visual overlays clearly delineating the two land cover types.

04

Area Quantification & Analysis

With the masks in place, pixel counts per class were computed and converted to approximate surface area estimates based on the satellite image's spatial resolution. This allowed for a quantitative comparison of biomass coverage versus bare land across the study area, which could be tracked over time for environmental monitoring purposes.

Results & Impact

The pipeline successfully produced clear segmentation masks distinguishing biomass-rich areas from zones of forage loss, using only the spectral information present in the satellite images — with no labelled training data required.

The value of this approach lies in its scalability and low cost. Traditional land cover surveys require field teams or expensive hyperspectral sensors. A K-Means based pipeline applied to freely available satellite imagery (e.g. from Sentinel-2 or Landsat) can produce comparable outputs at a fraction of the cost — making it accessible for conservation organisations, agricultural cooperatives, and government agencies with limited resources.

The same pipeline could be extended to track biomass changes over time by running the segmentation across multiple images captured at different dates, enabling temporal monitoring of land degradation or recovery.
